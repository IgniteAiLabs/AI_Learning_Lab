01What makes a prompt useful?

Give AI a role, your context, a clear goal and the method you want it to follow. For example: “Act as a friendly English coach for a Class 11 student. Ask me five grammar questions one at a time. Explain my mistakes after I answer.”

02What is a zero-shot prompt?

A zero-shot prompt asks for a task without showing an example of the desired answer. “Help me improve my English” is one example. It can still include detailed instructions; zero-shot describes the absence of examples, not the length of the prompt.

03Was the five-question coach prompt a few-shot prompt?

Strictly speaking, no examples of completed questions and answers were supplied, so the prompt demonstrated structured instructions rather than few-shot prompting. To make it few-shot, include one or more examples of the question-and-feedback style you want AI to copy.

04Why tell AI that I am in Class 11 at Hippocampus Rangapur?

That context helps AI choose suitable language, examples and question difficulty. If an answer is still too advanced, ask for simpler words or a more familiar example.

05Why ask for one question at a time?

It gives you time to think and answer independently. AI can wait, adapt to your response and explain the result before moving to the next question.

06Can I use the same prompt for Python instead of English?

Yes. Change the teacher’s subject, the topic and the difficulty; keep the one-question-at-a-time method and feedback instructions. Students did exactly this in the session, then changed “slightly challenging” to “easy” when the Python quiz was too difficult.

07What if AI’s question is too easy or too hard?

Say so explicitly: “Make the next five questions easier,” or “Focus on basic Python variables before loops.” You can also ask for a hint without requesting the full answer.

08What does it mean to use AI as a coach rather than a shortcut?

Try the task yourself first. Let AI ask questions, check your attempt, explain mistakes and offer another chance. Copying its answer without understanding skips the practice that helps you learn.

09Can AI make mistakes?

Yes. Review answers, test code and ask for the reasoning. The live quiz created a useful discussion about a grammar error; an AI score or explanation is still something you should check.

10How do I learn from a wrong answer?

Ask: “Why is my answer incorrect? Give me one contrasting example and let me try again.” Compare the two situations rather than merely memorising the corrected sentence.

11Can AI explain a topic in Telugu?

Yes. Ask it to explain the idea in Telugu, use simple words, give a short example, or translate a difficult term. Check that the explanation makes sense to you and ask a teacher when unsure.

12What if I learn at a different pace from a classmate?

That is normal. Ask for another example, a shorter explanation or extra practice. The bell-curve discussion was used to show that students need different amounts of support; it was not a way to label anyone.

13Can AI teach by asking questions instead of revealing answers?

Yes. Ask for a question-led or Socratic lesson: one question at a time, a hint after your attempt, and a short recap of what you understood and what needs practice. A student suggested this approach in the chat.

14Why is “had finished” correct after “by the time the principal arrived”?

The presentation was completed before the principal arrived. The past perfect form, had finished, marks the earlier past action: “By the time the principal arrived, the students had finished their presentation.”

15Why is it “If I had known,” not “If I knew,” about a missed meeting?

The meeting and missed opportunity are both in the past. Use if + had + past participle with would have + past participle: “If I had known about the meeting, I would have attended it.” “If I knew, I would attend” instead describes an imagined present or future situation.

16How do I change “I have completed my assignment” into reported speech?

“Meera said that she had completed her assignment.” The pronoun changes from I to she, and have completed usually moves back to had completed when reporting what she said.

17Why is it “Each of the players was” rather than “were”?

Each refers to one player at a time and takes a singular verb. “Along with their coach” adds information but does not change the subject: “Each of the players, along with their coach, was given a certificate.”

18Is the phrase “no sooner … then” or “no sooner … than”?

The fixed pair is no sooner … than: “No sooner had the bell rung than the students rushed out of the classroom.” Then usually refers to what happens next; than completes this construction.

19Is it “ring,” “rang” or “rung” after “had”?

Use the past participle rung after had. The forms are ring (base), rang (simple past) and rung (past participle).

20What does `print("Hello", name)` do?

If name = "Ravi", then print("Hello", name) displays Hello Ravi. The comma makes Python print the two values with a space between them.

21What if an AI-generated Python question is beyond my level?

Ask for beginner questions about variables, strings and print(). Ask AI to explain a line before showing the output, then run the code yourself to verify it.

22Can AI improve my self-introduction without changing who I am?

Write your own introduction first. Then ask AI to use simple, natural English, preserve your meaning and explain its three most important edits. Keep only phrases you would genuinely say.

23What should I include in a short self-introduction?

Start with your name and where you live. Add one thing you enjoy, one strength and something you hope to do in the future. These were the five starting points discussed in class.

24What are the four steps of the 30-day goal ladder?

My goal → why it matters → three small actions → how I will measure progress. Write your own version before comparing it with an AI plan.

25How could I measure progress in spoken English?

For example, speak for five minutes daily, learn three useful words a day and record one self-introduction each week. Compare four weekly recordings to hear what has changed. Three words a day is about 84 words over four weeks.

26What belongs on the AI Coach Starter Card?

Include: your strengths, a skill and career you are curious about, your program goal, a realistic 30-day plan, an improved prompt, one AI suggestion you used, one you changed or rejected, and a reflection on what you did and learned.

27Do I need to upload a handwritten photo for this assignment?

No. For this session, Kiran asked students to respond through the survey. A photograph of a handwritten page was not needed at this stage.

28What are the three exit-ticket sentences?

Complete: “Today I learned …”; “I was surprised by …”; and “Before tomorrow, I will …”. These brief responses help your mentor understand your progress and questions.

29What is the class AI honour code?

Use AI to learn, be honest about how you used it, respect other people’s ideas and give credit, protect privacy, create positive impact, and learn with effort and curiosity.

30What personal information should I keep out of AI chats?

Do not share passwords, OTPs, financial information, home addresses, private family details, or another person’s photos and documents without permission. Ask a trusted adult when unsure.

31If AI rewrites my work, what should I submit?

Show your original thinking alongside the improved version. Be clear about how AI helped, and check that you understand the final words. Give credit when using someone else’s ideas or work.

32What is the difference between AI and generative AI?

AI is a broad field of systems that perform tasks such as recognising patterns or making predictions. Generative AI is a part of AI that produces new content such as text, images, audio or code. The coffee-machine example in class made that distinction memorable.

33When did the field of AI begin, and why was 2022 mentioned?

The class used 1956 as a landmark year in the field’s history. 2022 marked the public release of ChatGPT and a sharp rise in public awareness of generative AI. AI research had been developing for decades before that.

34How can AI help teachers and learners?

Teachers can use it to draft lesson ideas and practice questions. Learners can ask for explanations at a suitable level, practise one question at a time and review feedback. Both should inspect its output for errors.

01What did students say they had learned in the first two days?

Students mentioned writing and using prompts, different prompt types, and using AI as a teacher or coach. The class began by asking everyone to finish “In the last two days, I learned …” with one new thing. Short answers were welcome, and there was no wrong answer to this reflection.

02What is the main learning cycle in this course?

Try a problem yourself, ask AI for help, check its answer, understand any mistake, improve your approach, and try again. Learning AI is like learning to ride a bicycle: you get better by actually doing it. Kiran revisited earlier topics so everyone could build confidence before moving on.

03Should I ask AI to create every prompt for me?

Start by expressing at least your own purpose and question. You may ask AI to help improve a draft, but deciding what you want to learn and thinking about the answer remain your work.

04What makes a prompt useful?

Give AI a role, your context, a clear goal, and the method you want it to follow. For example: “Act as a friendly English coach for a Class 11 student. Ask me five grammar questions, one at a time. Wait for each answer, then explain my mistakes.”

05Why ask for one question at a time?

It makes it easier to focus, attempt each question, and receive feedback before proceeding. Asking for all the questions and answers at once can make practice overwhelming and tempt you to skip the thinking.

06What is a zero-shot prompt?

It asks AI to do a task without giving an example of the desired answer. For example: “Explain a past participle to a Class 11 student.” A bare, vague request can be used as a shortcut; a zero-shot prompt can still be specific and useful if it gives enough context and asks you to participate.

07What is a few-shot prompt?

It includes a few examples that show AI the pattern you want it to follow. You can then compare its answer with your examples and improve the prompt. The class recalled both zero-shot and few-shot prompting; chain-of-thought prompting was mentioned but Kiran said it had not yet been covered.

08Can I reuse an English quiz prompt for Python?

Yes. Keep the one-question-at-a-time coaching method, but change the teacher role, subject, difficulty, and examples. Ask a beginner Python teacher for five suitable questions, wait for each response, and request feedback after you answer.

09Can AI help with English grammar and communication?

Yes. It can ask practice questions, explain your mistakes, and offer alternative ways to say something. Use the suggestions to strengthen your vocabulary and confidence while keeping your own natural voice. One earlier grammar example revisited in the session was the difference between “if I knew” and “if I had known” in an unreal past situation.

10Can AI explain a topic in Telugu?

Yes. Ask for a Telugu explanation or a bilingual explanation with a simple example. Kiran also considered making the learning FAQ available in Telugu so families could read it together; that was an idea discussed, not a completed feature announced in this session.

11Can AI make mistakes?

Yes. It may produce a convincing but false claim, sometimes called a hallucination. Check facts, examples, code, and calculations. If you disagree, ask why, consult your textbook, and ask a teacher or parent when needed.

12What should I do after I get a wrong answer?

Ask AI to explain the error, identify the idea you need to revise, and give another practice question. Then explain the correct reasoning in your own words. A mistake is a starting point for learning, not a reason to stop.

13How can I use AI with my textbook?

Give the topic and your level, then ask for a different explanation or an example of a maths or physics concept you find difficult. Compare the explanation with your textbook and teacher's guidance before relying on it.

14What is the difference between an AI coach and an AI shortcut?

An AI coach helps you practise, reflect, and improve. A shortcut gives you an answer to copy without understanding it. You should be able to explain every answer you submit in your own words.

## B. The learning FAQ, GitHub, and the poster

15Why did Kiran make a searchable FAQ website?

Students can quickly find a topic they missed or want to revise, including prompts, English, Python, and learning with AI. They can revisit a single question later instead of reading every page of a long summary. Students said it was easy to recap and catch up on missed points.

16How do I search the website?

Type a keyword such as “prompt” or “English,” or select a topic button. As you type, the displayed questions narrow down. The example website then contained 34 questions, including questions on English, prompting, and Python; those counts describe the demonstration, not a permanent limit.

17Will the website work on a phone and can I show my family?

Kiran demonstrated that its layout adapts to a phone or laptop. This is called responsive web design. He encouraged students to share the link with their parents and explain what they learned. Availability over time depends on the site continuing to be hosted and the link remaining valid.

18Can I choose which parts to revise?

Yes. Pick the questions you missed, forgot, or want to explore. Kiran said the longer session notes also contained more detail, but students did not need to read all ten pages at once. Follow your curiosity and practise the parts that matter to you.

19What is GitHub, in simple words?

It is a place where developers keep and share projects, especially code, and collaborate on changes. 20What is a repository?

A repository is a project's collection of files and its change history. The session showed separate repositories for different purposes and briefly showed the website's build and deployment status. Kiran said he would introduce website creation step by step later, after the initial weeks of learning.

21What does a `github.io` address indicate?

It usually identifies a website published through GitHub Pages. Kiran explained that the account name and project name form part of his learning site's address. The transcript did not preserve the full, exact URL, so use the link he shared in the class or WhatsApp group.

22Does `https://` mean a website is safe to trust?

HTTPS encrypts the connection to the site, which is useful, but it does not prove that a site or its content is trustworthy. Check the actual domain and avoid entering personal information on an unfamiliar site. Students may need a parent or teacher's help with account creation and publication; follow each service's actual age and account rules.

23How was the learning website created so quickly?

Kiran used AI to help turn the previous session transcript and chat into questions and answers, then made and published a web page. 24What was the bell-curve activity about?

Kiran counted how often each student participated in the chat and grouped the counts: 0–4 messages: 3 students; 5–9: 10; 10–14: 4; 15–19: 3; 20–24: 0. He used this distribution as a visual introduction to the bell shape and added a Telugu label to help families recognise it. These grouped counts alone do not establish that the data statistically follow a normal distribution, and message counts alone do not measure learning quality.

25What did the chat analysis help Kiran understand?

The chat gave him evidence of participation, questions, and points that might need more explanation. He used AI to count interactions, summarise the session, and draft the FAQ and poster. ## C. The AI honour code and classroom participation

27What are the six promises in the AI honour code?
  1. I try first: Attempt the problem before asking AI.
  2. I learn, then write: Understand the idea and answer in my own words.
  3. I check facts: Verify claims, examples, and calculations.
  4. I give credit: Acknowledge the people and sources that helped me.
  5. I protect privacy: Keep my own and others' private information safe.
  6. I own my work: Be able to explain every answer I submit.
28Whose privacy should I protect?

Your own, your classmates', your friends', your family's, and the school's. Do not share someone else's sensitive information simply to ask AI a question or post an example.

29How can I participate if I am shy or unsure?

Use the chat, an emoji, the raise-hand button, or your microphone. Ask for repetition or say when a topic is too fast or boring. Kiran asked students to share what surprised them and what they planned to do before the next class so he could adjust the teaching.

30Is it okay to make mistakes or take longer on a project?

Yes. The class is a place to practise. Kiran encouraged students to share incomplete work when ready and reminded everyone that the Scratch projects were not a competition. Students were invited to appreciate one another's work with stars and comments.

31What should I tell my parents or teachers about the class?

Explain one thing you learned, one thing that surprised you, and one thing you will try next. Show them a topic on the FAQ website if helpful. You can also tell your teacher which topic needs more time, and you can teach a younger student a responsible prompting technique you understand.

## D. Scratch, music, and Code.org

32What is Scratch and why learn it before or alongside Python?

Scratch is a browser-based environment for making programs by joining visual blocks. It gives beginners a way to practise sequences, events, and experimentation, which can help when they later write Python. The correct site is scratch.mit.edu; the spoken address was mistranscribed. An internet connection lets you try the website without installing desktop software.

34What happens when I change the tempo?

A higher tempo generally makes the tune play faster; a lower tempo makes it slower. Kiran demonstrated values around 185, 50, and 85, returning to roughly 85 for the student's version. The values were part of an experiment, not universal settings for every song.

35What happens when I change the instrument?

The same note sequence can sound different as piano, guitar, flute, saxophone, clarinet, or electric piano. Kiran tried several sounds and preferred piano for the example. Change one setting at a time and listen to what changes.

36How are notes represented in the Scratch music example?

The blocks used numbers for pitches and values for how long a note should play. In the simple example Kiran identified 60 as middle C and began “Twinkle, Twinkle” with repeated C notes followed by G notes. 38What is Code.org and what was demonstrated?

Kiran said the planned AI topics would unfold over the next four weeks and that AI-assisted music composition could be explored afterward. The Scratch and Code.org demonstrations were introductions and optional areas to investigate during the weekend.

10What is the difference between a draft and a submission?

A draft has one or more answers saved while you are still working. A submission is the completed form sent after all required sections are answered. 13What is the difference between AI and generative AI?

AI is a broad field of systems that perform tasks associated with intelligence, such as recognizing patterns, making predictions or solving problems. Generative AI is a part of AI that creates new text, images, audio, code or other content from a request. Haroon described AI in chat as computer technology for tasks such as learning, reasoning and problem solving. An everyday example makes the distinction clearer than memorizing the terms alone.

14What does it mean to use AI as a coach rather than a shortcut?

A coach explains a difficult idea, asks you a question, points out an error and lets you try again. A shortcut does the assignment for you while you skip the thinking. Sanjay’s chat example made the distinction concrete: ask AI to explain the steps of a math problem so you can solve a similar one, instead of copying an entire AI-written history essay as your own. 15May I use AI while completing this assessment?

Use your own understanding for the assessment so the mentor can see what you have learned. You may use AI afterward to revisit a topic, ask why an answer was incomplete and practise another example. 16What are role, context, goal and method in a prompt?

Role tells AI the kind of help wanted, such as a patient tutor. Context explains who you are and what you already know. Goal says what you want to achieve. Method or output specifies how to respond, such as one question at a time, simple English, a table or a printable plan. The lesson showed that these details can turn a vague request into guidance suited to a Class 11 student.

17Why did changing “slightly challenging Python questions” to “easy questions” matter?

That small change told the AI to reduce the level of difficulty. You can adjust a prompt when practice is too easy or too hard: state your present level, ask for one question at a time, attempt it yourself and request a hint before a full solution. The goal is progress at a manageable pace, not just receiving more answers.

05What problem is the proposed pet tracking project trying to solve?

06What is a guide dog, and why would losing one be serious?

A guide dog is trained to help a person with a visual impairment navigate everyday places. If the dog and handler become separated, the person may lose an important source of mobility and reassurance. A tracking design should begin by listening to such users and understanding how they would receive help, rather than assuming a gadget alone solves the problem.

07Are QR, NFC, RFID and Bluetooth beacons the same thing?

No. A QR code is a printed code somebody must scan. NFC usually needs a reader very close to the tag. RFID uses a compatible reader and its useful distance depends on the tag and setup. A Bluetooth beacon can be detected by nearby compatible devices. Each can help answer a different question; none automatically provides continuous, worldwide GPS location.

08Can an ordinary tag tell us exactly where a pet is at every moment?

Usually not. A location estimate needs a way to detect the tag and send information to the owner. A QR code tells you about a scan; an NFC or RFID system tells you about detection by a nearby reader; a beacon needs compatible devices in range. A boundary alert works only when the chosen system can reliably detect entry or exit. The team must test coverage, delay, battery life and privacy before promising real-time tracking.

10What is the Internet of Things, and where does Python fit?

The Internet of Things, or IoT, connects physical devices and their data to software. In a pet tracking prototype, a tag or reader might produce observations, and software could display them or decide when to notify someone. Python could help clean data, process events or build a simple demonstration. First define the problem and the data you need; then choose the hardware and code.

26What is the difference between zero-shot and few-shot prompting?

32What is an AI hallucination, and how do I check an answer?

A hallucination is a confident-sounding answer that contains invented or incorrect details. A model knowing an author’s pronoun, a claimed location, a citation or a Python solution does not prove that the detail is right.
